## Biography

Andrew J. Millis received his AB in physics from Harvard (1982) and his PhD from MIT (1986). He spent about ten years as a theorist at Bell Laboratories, then held professorships at Johns Hopkins University and Rutgers University before joining Columbia in 2001, where he chaired the physics department from 2006 to 2009. He became associate director for physics at the Simons Foundation in 2011 and directed the Simons Collaboration on the Many Electron Problem, moving to the Flatiron Institute in 2017 as co-director of the new Center for Computational Quantum Physics. He is a member of the US National Academy of Sciences (2020), a Fellow of the APS and AAAS, and received the Hamburg Prize for Theoretical Physics (2017) and the 2026 John Bardeen Prize (Simons Foundation; Columbia Physics).

## Research footprint

Millis works on the theory of electrons in materials, with emphasis on collective phenomena such as superconductivity and magnetism. His early work includes large-N treatments of heavy-fermion (Kondo lattice) systems and the influential theory of itinerant quantum critical points (the Hertz–Millis framework). In the 1990s he showed, with Littlewood and Shraiman, that double exchange alone cannot explain resistivity in colossal-magnetoresistance manganites, emphasising electron–lattice coupling. He became a major developer and user of dynamical mean-field theory and its extensions, applying them to cuprate and nickelate superconductors, transition-metal oxides and oxide heterostructures, and later to non-equilibrium and driven correlated systems. At Flatiron he has promoted algorithm development for the many-electron problem. His 2026 arXiv output covers bilayer nickelates, SrTiO₃ superconductivity with GW methods, impurity quantum criticality and entanglement witnesses, quantum geometry and Wigner crystals in moiré systems, cavity-modified cuprate superconductivity and topological Kondo insulators.
