## Biography

Analytis took a BSc in physics at the University of Canterbury, New Zealand (2001). As a Rhodes Scholar he completed a DPhil at Oxford (2006), working with Stephen Blundell and Arzhang Ardavan on quasi-two-dimensional organic superconductors. He was a Lloyd's Tercentenary Fellow at the University of Bristol, where he studied anisotropic scattering in cuprates. From 2008 he was a postdoc at Stanford, working on iron-pnictide superconductors and topological insulators. He joined the Berkeley faculty in January 2013 as Charles Kittel Chair. He was Department Chair from 2020 to 2023 and in that role created the Pi2 undergraduate fellowship and the Physics Innovation Lab (Berkeley page). Honours beyond these were not retrieved.

## Research footprint

Analytis is a materials-discovery experimentalist who grows crystals and probes them with transport, often in high magnetic fields and under strain. His lab page states he uses "materials as a tool to get to scientifically important problems by designing and tuning their properties". At Stanford, with Ian Fisher and Jiun-Haw Chu, he co-authored work on in-plane resistivity anisotropy (*Science* 2010) and divergent nematic susceptibility (*Science* 2012) in iron arsenides. These papers helped establish electronic nematicity in iron-based superconductors. He also did early quantum-oscillation and transport work on topological-insulator surface states. At Berkeley he has studied quantum critical transport, Kitaev-type honeycomb iridates and chiral magnets. He also works on electrical switching of antiferromagnets in intercalated transition-metal dichalcogenides such as Fe1/3NbS2 (Nair et al., *Nature Materials* 2019). His three stated questions are: whether quantum criticality is universal, how topology shapes interactions, and whether quantum properties can be stabilised at ambient conditions.

## Public statements and values

From his Berkeley page: "Our understanding of condensed matter physics is driven by the discovery of new materials." He notes that few would have expected oxides to host such rich quantum properties before the cuprates, and that high-mobility GaAs was needed before the fractional quantum Hall effect could be seen.
