## Biography

Nima Arkani-Hamed was born in 1972 in Houston to Iranian physicist parents. His family lived in Iran after 1979, fled to Canada in the early 1980s, and he grew up there. He earned a joint honours degree in mathematics and physics at the University of Toronto (1993) and a PhD at UC Berkeley (1997) under Lawrence Hall. After a postdoc at SLAC, he joined the Berkeley faculty (1999) and was a professor at Harvard (2002–2008). He moved to IAS in 2008 and became inaugural Feinberg Director of its Cross-Disciplinary Program in 2021. He also directs the Center for Future High Energy Physics in Beijing. His honours include the Gribov Medal (2003), the Sackler Prize (2008), the inaugural Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics (2012) and the Sakurai Prize (2021). He was elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (2009) and the US National Academy of Sciences (2017) (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

His most cited work, with Savas Dimopoulos and Gia Dvali (1998), proposed that large extra dimensions could explain the weakness of gravity; a related paper with Antoniadis added TeV-scale strings. At Harvard he developed dimensional deconstruction and electroweak symmetry breaking from it (with Cohen and Georgi, 2001), "little Higgs" models (2002), split supersymmetry (with Dimopoulos, 2004) and the weak gravity conjecture (with Motl, Nicolis and Vafa, 2006). With Finkbeiner, Slatyer and Weiner he proposed "A Theory of Dark Matter" (2008). Since about 2009 he has led the geometric reformulation of scattering amplitudes: twistor-space S-matrix (2009), positive Grassmannian and the amplituhedron (from 2013). Later work covers cosmological correlators and the "cosmohedron" (2026), and links to combinatorics and representation theory. He continues to work on phenomenology, including flavour hierarchies with Lawrence Hall (2026) and future colliders (CEPC detector report, 2025).

## Public statements and values

On his IAS homepage he writes that the Standard Model plus general relativity is "in spectacular agreement with all known experiments" but "almost certainly fundamentally incomplete". In a 2016 Cornell talk, "The Morality of Fundamental Physics", he said he is an atheist but "not a militant atheist", and that "we can devote ourselves to this slow unveiling of the vastness of truth in all of its glory" (Wikipedia). He is a prominent public advocate of next-generation colliders and appeared in the 2013 documentary *Particle Fever* (Wikipedia; Quanta Magazine 2015).
