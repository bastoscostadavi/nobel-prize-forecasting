## Biography

Born 15 June 1947 in Agen, France. He studied at ENS Cachan, passed the agrégation in physics (1969), and received a doctorate from the École supérieure d'optique at Orsay (1971). He then taught in Cameroon for three years in place of military service. His doctorat d'État (Paris-Sud, 1983) reported his Bell-inequality experiments. He became a CNRS research director at the Institut d'Optique and a professor at École polytechnique. His honours include the Wolf Prize (2010), the Albert Einstein Medal (2012), the Niels Bohr International Gold Medal (2013), the Balzan Prize (2013) and the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics (with Clauser and Zeilinger). He is a member of the French Academy of Sciences and the French Academy of Technologies, a Foreign Member of the Royal Society (2015), and a foreign associate of the US NAS. He was elected to the Académie française in 2025 (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Aspect's 1980–82 experiments at Orsay used entangled photon pairs from atomic cascades, and later added fast-switching polarisers. They violated Bell inequalities with high significance and addressed the locality loophole. This work made entanglement a laboratory resource. After working with Claude Cohen-Tannoudji on laser cooling below the recoil limit, he founded the Atom Optics group at the Institut d'Optique. There his group worked on Bose-Einstein condensates, atom lasers, Anderson localisation of matter waves, and the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect with atoms. He has also worked on single-photon sources and wave-particle duality experiments, including a version of Wheeler's delayed-choice experiment. He regularly describes his field's history as a "second quantum revolution" built on entanglement and single-particle control.

## Public statements and values

- "When I did these experiments testing Bell's inequalities, at the end the conclusion is yes, quantum mechanics resists all possible attacks" (NobelPrize.org telephone interview, 2022). The same page reports that he had been trying to find the limit of quantum mechanics and "didn't find it".
- The same interview stresses the need for constant questioning in physics.
