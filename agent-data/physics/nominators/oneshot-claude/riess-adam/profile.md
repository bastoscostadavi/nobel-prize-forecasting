## Biography

Born 16 December 1969 in Washington, D.C., and raised in New Jersey. He received a B.S. from MIT (1992) and a PhD from Harvard (1996), advised by Robert Kirshner and William Press. He was a Miller Fellow at UC Berkeley (1996–99), then joined STScI in 1999 and Johns Hopkins in 2006. His honours include the Warner Prize (2003), the Shaw Prize (2006), a MacArthur Fellowship (2008), the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics (half, with Perlmutter; the other half shared with Schmidt), the Albert Einstein Medal (2011) and the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics (2015). He was elected to the US NAS in 2009 and is an AAS Fellow (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

As a member of the High-Z Supernova Search Team co-led by Brian Schmidt, Riess led the 1998 analysis showing that distant Type Ia supernovae were fainter than expected. This was evidence that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, and it pointed to dark energy or a cosmological constant. He then led the Higher-Z team (2002–07), which used Hubble Space Telescope supernovae to show an earlier phase of deceleration. Since 2005 he has led the SH0ES programme, which refines the Cepheid–supernova distance ladder to measure the Hubble constant with about 1% precision. Its results disagree with the value predicted from the cosmic microwave background under standard ΛCDM. This "Hubble tension" is now a central debate in cosmology, and his recent work uses HST and JWST to test the ladder's systematics.

## Public statements and values

- On the 1998 result: "I remember seeing that the sign of the answer was, I would have said, wrong... 'Uh, I've made a terrible mistake'... and then spending weeks looking for it" (NobelPrize.org telephone interview, 2011).
- On the rival Supernova Cosmology Project, competition "lent a sense of urgency". When he learned they saw the same thing, "it went from 'Oh, this is a terrible mistake' to 'Oh my God, this might be the right answer!'" (same).
- On dark energy: "I wish we did [know]... They didn't give us the Nobel Prize for that!" He said progress would come from "more experiments, and hopefully somebody else with some great ideas" (same).
