## Biography

Basov took an MSc in experimental physics in the USSR in 1987. He then earned a PhD in physics in 1991 at the Lebedev Physics Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences. He was a professor at the University of California, San Diego from 1997 to 2016 and chaired its physics department from 2010 to 2015. He joined Columbia in 2016, where he is Higgins Professor of Physics. He directs the DOE Energy Frontier Research Center on Programmable Quantum Materials and co-directs the Max Planck–New York City Center for Nonequilibrium Quantum Phenomena. His honours include a Sloan Fellowship (1999), a Humboldt Research Award (2009), the APS Frank Isakson Prize (2012), the Genzel Prize (2014), Moore Investigator awards (2014, 2020), the K. J. Button Prize (2019) and a DoD Vannevar Bush Faculty Fellowship (2019). He was elected to the NAS in 2020, and he is also a member of Academia Europaea (Simons; Columbia news; ae-info).

## Research footprint

Basov's work centres on the electrodynamics of quantum materials, which he probes with infrared light. In his early career he did far-infrared and optical spectroscopy of high-temperature cuprate superconductors and other correlated-electron systems. From there he broadened to a general programme on the optical response of correlated matter. Later he pioneered infrared nano-imaging and nano-spectroscopy based on scattering-type near-field microscopy. His group used these tools to image nanoscale phase coexistence and to visualise plasmon, phonon and exciton polaritons in van der Waals materials such as graphene and hexagonal boron nitride. He describes his interests as "physics of quantum materials, superconductivity, two-dimensional materials and infrared nano-optics" (Simons). His 2026 preprints continue these lines:
- engineering vacuum fluctuations in hyperbolic heterostructures;
- antiferromagnetism-altered plasmon dynamics;
- quantitative infrared nanoscopy;
- a Colloquium on semi-Dirac fermions;
- "quantum light nano-imaging".

The trajectory runs from bulk spectroscopy of correlated electrons, to nanoscale imaging of light–matter hybrids, to "programmable" quantum materials and cavity control.
