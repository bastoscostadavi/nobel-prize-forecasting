## Biography

Born 14 September 1951 in London. He was educated at St Paul's School and Christ's College, Cambridge, where he received his BA and PhD (1978) under Philip W. Anderson. He held positions at the Institut Laue–Langevin in Grenoble (1977–81), the University of Southern California (1981–87) and UC San Diego (1986–92), and has been at Princeton since 1990. He was a Distinguished Visiting Research Chair at Perimeter Institute (2013–18). His honours include the Oliver E. Buckley Prize (1993), the ICTP Dirac Medal (2012), and the 2016 Nobel Prize in Physics (half, with Thouless and Kosterlitz sharing the other half). He is a Fellow of the Royal Society (1996) and a member of the US NAS (2017). He holds British and Slovenian citizenship (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Haldane's work is behind several central ideas of modern condensed matter theory. In one dimension he formulated the Luttinger-liquid concept and predicted the "Haldane gap" in integer-spin Heisenberg chains (1983), an early example of what are now called topological phases. His Nobel biography recounts that this paper was rejected by multiple journals before later numerical studies and experiments confirmed it. In the quantum Hall effect he introduced Haldane pseudopotentials and the hierarchy construction. His 1988 honeycomb-lattice model showed a quantum Hall effect without Landau levels (the quantum anomalous Hall effect), which anticipated Chern insulators and topological materials. Later contributions include exclusion statistics, entanglement spectra (with Li), photonic topological states (with Raghu), and geometric descriptions of fractional quantum Hall fluids.

## Public statements and values

- "I think it is a very bad thing when government agencies start to say... we should never say things like 'What's it used for?'" because big useful discoveries "occur because someone discovers something interesting and it turns out to be tremendously useful" (NobelPrize.org telephone interview, 2016).
- "The surprise in everything is that quantum mechanics is so much richer than we dreamed" (same).
- "It's very difficult to know whether something is useful or not, but one can know that it's exciting" (same).
- In his Nobel biography he recalls a referee claiming his spin-chain predictions "were in manifest contradiction to fundamental principles such as renormalization and continuity", and notes that they were later vindicated.
