## Biography

Elisa Resconi was born on 1 December 1971 in Brescia, Italy. She studied physics at the University of Milan (1989–1995) and received her PhD from the University of Genoa in 2001, supervised by G. Manuzio and R. Raghavan, with a thesis on solar neutrinos at Gran Sasso. She was a Marie Curie Fellow at DESY Zeuthen (2002–2005) and led an Emmy Noether group at the Max Planck Institute for Nuclear Physics in Heidelberg (2005–2010/11). After a guest professorship at Erlangen-Nuremberg, she was appointed at TUM in 2012 (Wikipedia gives a Heisenberg Professorship in 2013–2016). In 2018 she received a Liesel Beckmann Distinguished Professorship. She has been spokesperson of DFG SFB 1258 "Neutrinos and Dark Matter in Astro- and Particle Physics" since 2017. She holds an ERC Advanced Grant ("Neutrinoshot", 2022) and was elected P-ONE spokesperson in 2023.

## Research footprint

Resconi moved from solar-neutrino detection to high-energy neutrino astronomy with IceCube. Her TUM page highlights "Dissecting the region around IceCube-170922A: the blazar TXS 0506+056 as the first cosmic neutrino source" (Padovani, Giommi & Resconi) together with the IceCube Science papers on TXS 0506+056 (2018). She is also associated with the IceCube evidence for neutrino emission from the Seyfert galaxy NGC 1068 (Science 2022). Methodological contributions include the atmospheric self-veto concept ("Vetoing atmospheric neutrinos in a high energy neutrino telescope", Schönert, Gaisser, Resconi & Schulz). She co-wrote the textbook *Cosmic Rays and Particle Physics* (2nd ed., 2016) with Thomas Gaisser and Ralph Engel. She now leads P-ONE, a planned deep-sea neutrino telescope on Ocean Networks Canada's NEPTUNE infrastructure ("The Pacific Ocean Neutrino Experiment", Nature Astronomy 2020).

## Public statements and values

Her TUM page states that "cosmic neutrinos, as new astrophysical messengers, open a new window onto the universe", allowing exploration of "phenomena in cosmic accelerators and collapsing stars that would be otherwise inaccessible." Wikipedia notes that she advocates new neutrino observatories to complement IceCube and KM3NeT.
