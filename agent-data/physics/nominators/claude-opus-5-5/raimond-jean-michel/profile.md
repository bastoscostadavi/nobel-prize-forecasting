## Biography

Jean-Michel Raimond was born on 11 December 1955 in Orléans, the son of the literary scholar Michel Raimond. He attended Lycée Louis-le-Grand and entered ENS (rue d'Ulm) in 1975, earning the agrégation in physics. He joined the CNRS and worked in Serge Haroche's laboratory at ENS (later Laboratoire Kastler Brossel), completing a third-cycle thesis in 1979 and a thèse d'État in 1983/84 on Rydberg atoms in resonant cavities. From 1988 he was professor at Université Paris VI (now Sorbonne Université). He was a junior (1994–1999) and senior (2001–2011) member of the Institut Universitaire de France, and headed the ENS physics department from 2004 to 2009. Honours: Prix Fernand Holweck (1985), Grand Prix Ampère (1998, with Michel Brune), Prix Jean Ricard (2007), Gay-Lussac-Humboldt Prize (2012), EPS Edison-Volta Prize (2014), Academia Europaea (2011), Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur.

## Research footprint

Raimond helped build the ENS cavity-QED programme with Haroche and Brune. Circular Rydberg atoms cross superconducting microwave cavities with very long photon lifetimes, which makes it possible to study atom–field interaction at the single-quantum level. Key verified works:

- "Observing the Progressive Decoherence of the 'Meter' in a Quantum Measurement" (Brune et al., PRL 1996), which followed decoherence of mesoscopic field superpositions.
- "Quantum jumps of light recording the birth and death of a photon in a cavity" (Gleyzes et al., Nature 2007).
- "Reconstruction of non-classical cavity field states with snapshots of their decoherence" (Deléglise et al., Nature 2008).
- "Real-time quantum feedback prepares and stabilizes photon number states" (Sayrin et al., Nature 2011).

With Haroche he wrote the monograph *Exploring the Quantum: Atoms, Cavities, and Photons* (Oxford University Press, 2006). The team now pursues quantum simulation with laser-trapped circular Rydberg atoms of rubidium and strontium (LKB page).

## Public statements and values

English Wikipedia attributes to him, about the 1996 experiment, the statement that wave-function collapse was observed "for the first time" using quantum-mechanical methods (the attribution is Wikipedia's paraphrase and was not checked against a primary source). His work and teaching (ENS lecture notes are hosted by the department) stress quantum mechanics as an experimental science of measurement, decoherence and control (inference).
