My best guess is **Michael Berry and Yakir Aharonov**, for their discoveries concerning **quantum phases**, particularly the Berry phase and the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

Their work revealed how a particle’s quantum behavior can depend on the geometry of its evolution and on electromagnetic potentials. These ideas now underpin much of our understanding of quantum interference and topological materials. They have the combination Nobel committees often reward: fundamental insight, extensive experimental confirmation, and lasting influence.

It’s a **low-confidence prediction**, though. Another plausible choice is **Peter Shor**, for quantum algorithms and quantum error correction. Nobel selections are difficult to forecast, and neither scientific importance nor a long wait guarantees a prize.
