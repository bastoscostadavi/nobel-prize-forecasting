## Biography

Born 1953 (Swedish Wikipedia). PhD at Stockholm University in 1982, with a thesis on perturbative phenomena in diatomic molecules studied by time-resolved spectroscopy. He then worked at KTH before returning to Stockholm University as professor of molecular physics; Wikidata/ORCID give 1996 onward. Göran Gustafsson Prize in physics (1996). Elected to the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, Class for physics, in 2009. Director of AlbaNova University Center from 2011, succeeding Ulf Wahlgren. He served on the Nobel Committee for Physics, finishing his ninth year in 2023, and was listed as co-opted in 2022. Former chair of the atomic and molecular physics section of the Swedish Physical Society.

## Research footprint

His central theme is how molecular ions interact with low-energy electrons, especially dissociative recombination, and its role in the chemistry of interstellar clouds and planetary ionospheres. He used the CRYRING heavy-ion storage ring in Stockholm for many such measurements (*Studies of Dissociative Recombination in CRYRING*, 2003) and co-authored the monograph *Dissociative Recombination of Molecular Ions* with Ann E. Orel (Cambridge University Press, 2008). Methods span accelerators, storage rings and laser techniques. His stated interests also include free-electron-laser studies of small molecules and, more recently, molecular chirality and chiral interactions; he chaired a Nobel Symposium on chiral matter planned for 2020, with Dmitri Kharzeev as co-chair. He has also acted as a scientific expert in the defence of Swedish athletes in several doping cases.

## Public statements and values

From his 2023 article in *The Conversation*:
- Committee work is "an absolutely fascinating job – albeit hard"; committee discussions are "open, sharp, frank and sometimes emotional, but never hostile."
- He stresses the historical point that relativity itself "took until 2020… to be awarded explicitly", while quantum mechanics was "richly awarded".
- He describes the process as rigorous and expert-driven, relying on confidential reports on whether discoveries are "of sufficient importance".

His public activity includes explaining the Nobel selection process to schools and at symposia.
