## Biography

Mavalvala was born in 1968 in Lahore, Pakistan, and raised mainly in Karachi (Wikipedia). She moved to the United States in 1986 and earned a BA in physics and astronomy from Wellesley College in 1990. She received her PhD from MIT in 1997 under Rainer Weiss, with a thesis on alignment issues in laser-interferometric gravitational-wave detectors. She was a postdoc and research scientist at Caltech, working first on the cosmic microwave background and then on LIGO. She joined the MIT physics faculty in 2002, later became Associate Department Head, and was appointed Dean of the School of Science in 2020. Her honours include a Sloan Fellowship (2005), a MacArthur Fellowship (2010), APS Fellowship (2010), the APS Joseph F. Keithley Award (2013), Optica Fellowship (2014), and shares of the 2016 Special Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics and Gruber Cosmology Prize as a LIGO member. She was elected to the US National Academy of Sciences in 2017.

## Research footprint

She has worked on gravitational-wave interferometry since 1991 and was part of the LIGO team that made the first detection of gravitational waves, announced in 2016. Her group's signature contribution is the generation and injection of squeezed states of light into LIGO's kilometre-scale interferometers. This reduced quantum noise and improved detector sensitivity. Her group was also the first to produce squeezed light by optomechanics at room temperature. A parallel line extends laser cooling to ever larger objects: cooling a centimetre-scale mirror to 0.8 K and observing a 2.7 kg pendulum near its quantum ground state. The aim is to observe quantum behaviour in human-scale objects (Wikipedia). The focus combines precision measurement, quantum optics and astrophysics.
