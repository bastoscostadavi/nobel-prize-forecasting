## Biography

Ian Alexander Walmsley was born in 1960. He was educated at Imperial College London and at the Institute of Optics, University of Rochester. At Oxford he held the Hooke Professorship of Experimental Physics, was a professorial fellow of St Hugh's College and was Pro-Vice-Chancellor for Research (from 2009). He directed the NQIT hub of the UK National Quantum Technologies Programme. He was Provost of Imperial College London from 1 September 2018 and returned to Oxford in 2025 to lead the Oxford Quantum Institute. He chairs the National Quantum Computing Centre Strategic Advisory Board and sits on the UK National Quantum Strategic Advisory Board. His honours include FRS (2012), the APS Joseph F. Keithley Award (2011) and a CBE (2024, "for services to science and quantum technologies"). He is a Fellow of the IOP, APS and Optica. In 2019 he co-founded ORCA Computing.

## Research footprint

Walmsley's early reputation rests on ultrafast optics. With Chris Iaconis he invented SPIDER, "Spectral phase interferometry for direct electric-field reconstruction of ultrashort optical pulses" (Optics Letters 1998), a standard technique for characterizing ultrashort pulses. He later moved into quantum optics: engineering the spectral structure of photon pairs, broadband Raman quantum memories ("Towards high-speed optical quantum memories", Reim et al., Nature Photonics 2010), and photonic quantum information. His group reported one of the first experimental boson-sampling demonstrations, "Boson Sampling on a Photonic Chip" (Spring et al., Science 2013). Later work covered Gaussian boson sampling and networked photonic quantum computing. His Oxford page summarizes his research as "the application of light to quantum information processing, from simulation and computing to sensors and networks." His career has increasingly combined research with national quantum-technology leadership.

## Public statements and values

On his OQI appointment (Oxford, May 2025) he said the institute would "address both foundational questions in quantum science and application challenges in quantum technologies" and "push forward the boundaries of both discovery science and technology." He also said it would position Oxford "as a thought leader and convener for industry, government and civil society."
