## Biography

Egor Babaev was born in Leningrad in 1973. INSPIRE lists his undergraduate training at the Ioffe Physico-Technical Institute. He received a PhD in theoretical physics from Uppsala University in 2001. Wikipedia names Antti Niemi, Ludwig Faddeev and Neil Ashcroft as his advisors; INSPIRE lists Niemi. He joined the KTH faculty in 2006 and is now a full professor of theoretical physics there. From 2007 to 2013 he also held a position at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. His honours include the Göran Gustafsson Prize and the Tage Erlander Prize, both awarded by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. He also received an Outstanding Young Researcher award from the Swedish Research Council, an NSF CAREER award, and Fellowship of the American Physical Society "for pioneering contributions to the theory of multicomponent superconductors and superfluids" (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Babaev's work centres on superconductors and superfluids with more than one order-parameter component. In 2002 he proposed that multicomponent superconductors can host fractional vortices, which carry only part of a flux quantum (TDLI summary). A Stanford-led experiment published in *Science* in 2023, which he co-authored, reported vortices whose flux fraction varies with temperature. He and his collaborators developed the concept of "type-1.5" superconductivity, a regime in multiband systems that lies between the classic type-I and type-II behaviours. Other contributions include:
- knotted solitons (hopfions), through the Babaev–Faddeev–Niemi line of work;
- theories of metallic superfluids and superconducting superfluids in ultra-compressed hydrogen and its alloys;
- the "quartic metal" (2021), a proposed phase above the superconducting transition with broken time-reversal symmetry.

He co-authored a textbook on superfluid states of matter with Boris Svistunov and Nikolay Prokof'ev. His methods combine field theory, topological soliton analysis and large-scale numerical simulation. The KTH page notes that his group has more recently joined international collaborations on superconducting quantum-computing hardware. That points to a shift towards applications, although his core work remains foundational.
