## Biography

Steinberg received a B.S. from Yale (1988) and a Ph.D. in physics from UC Berkeley (1994) under Raymond Chiao. His thesis, "When can light go faster than light? The tunneling time and its sub-femtosecond measurement via quantum interference," won an APS thesis award. He joined the University of Toronto in 1996 and was a founding member of its Centre for Quantum Information and Quantum Control. He started a CIFAR quantum information program in 2002. His honours include the CAP Herzberg Medal and the Royal Society of Canada's Rutherford Memorial Medal (both 2006), an E.W.R. Steacie Fellowship (2007), and fellowships of the APS (2008), Optica (2009) and the Royal Society of Canada (2016). He was named University Professor at Toronto in 2021 (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Steinberg works where quantum optics meets the foundations of quantum mechanics. His doctoral work with Chiao and Kwiat measured single-photon tunneling times and the apparently "superluminal" group delays involved. At Toronto he built a program in weak measurement. The best-known result is Kocsis et al., *Science* 332 (2011), which reconstructed the average trajectories of single photons in a two-slit interferometer. *Physics World* named it Breakthrough of the Year for 2011. He returned to tunneling with ultracold rubidium atoms and used a Larmor-clock scheme to measure how long an atom spends inside a barrier (Ramos et al., *Nature* 583, 2020). Other work covers multiphoton entanglement, quantum state tomography, measurement beyond the Rayleigh criterion, and quantum information stored in cold atomic gases. His focus has moved over time from photon tunneling (1990s), to entangled-photon and measurement experiments (2000s–2010s), to cold-atom tests of quantum dynamics.

## Connections

- Doctoral advisor: Raymond Chiao (Berkeley). Paul Kwiat was a co-author on the tunneling-time work.
- Co-authors on the 2011 trajectories paper include Kocsis, Braverman, Ravets, Stevens, Mirin and Shalm (NIST collaborators provided the quantum-dot/photon sources).
- On the 2020 tunneling paper: Ramos, Spierings and Racicot.
- Toronto quantum optics colleagues include Joseph Thywissen, Hoi-Kwong Lo, Daniel James, John Sipe and Boris Braverman, a former co-author who is now faculty.
- He has a long association with CIFAR's quantum information program.
