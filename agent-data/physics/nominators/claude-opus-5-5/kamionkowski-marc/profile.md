## Biography

Kamionkowski was born in Cleveland, Ohio, in 1965. He took a BA at Washington University in St. Louis (1987) and a PhD at the University of Chicago (1991) under Michael Turner. After a postdoc at the Institute for Advanced Study, he joined Columbia's physics faculty in 1994. He moved to Caltech, where he became Robinson Professor of Theoretical Physics and Astrophysics and was founding director of the Moore Center for Theoretical Cosmology and Physics. He joined Johns Hopkins in 2011 and has been Kenan Professor there since 2016. His honours include:
- the AAS Helen B. Warner Prize (1998);
- the DOE E. O. Lawrence Award (2006);
- the Dannie Heineman Prize for Astrophysics (2015);
- a Simons Investigator award (2014);
- the Gruber Cosmology Prize (2021, with Uroš Seljak and Matias Zaldarriaga).

He is a member of the NAS (2019) and a fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (2013), APS, AAS and ISGRG (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Kamionkowski first became known for supersymmetric dark matter, notably a widely used review of its phenomenology and detection. He also showed how precision measurements of the cosmic microwave background could determine cosmological parameters and the geometry of the universe. His best-known contribution may be the theory of CMB polarization. With colleagues he showed that primordial gravitational waves from inflation leave a distinctive curl ("B-mode") polarization pattern. That idea motivated a generation of polarization experiments and was recognized by the Lawrence Award and the Gruber Prize. He has also worked on:
- cosmic birefringence and parity violation;
- early dark energy as a response to the Hubble tension;
- primordial black holes as dark matter;
- 21-cm cosmology;
- the "total-angular-momentum" formalism for cosmological perturbations.

His 2026 preprints cover decaying dark matter constrained by CMB lensing, chiral dark photons, peculiar-velocity statistics and TAM-wave perturbation equations.

## Public statements and values

In March 2014 the BICEP2 team announced a B-mode detection, which was later attributed largely to Galactic dust. At the time he told BBC News: "This is spectacular... I've seen the research; the arguments are persuasive, and the scientists involved are among the most careful and conservative people I know." The quote shows his investment in the observational test his theory proposed, and that he places weight on the care of the experimenters. The DOE citation praised analyses showing "that precise observations of the cosmic microwave background can lead to deep understanding of the origin and evolution of the Universe, thereby motivating a series of increasingly precise cosmological experiments."
