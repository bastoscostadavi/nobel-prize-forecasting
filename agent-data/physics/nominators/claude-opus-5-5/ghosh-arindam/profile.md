## Biography

Ghosh earned a B.Sc. in physics (honours) from St. Xavier's College, Kolkata (University of Calcutta, 1991). He then did the Integrated PhD program at IISc, receiving an M.Sc. (1994) and PhD (1999). He was a postdoctoral research associate at the University of Cambridge (2000–2005), then returned to IISc as Assistant Professor (2005–2011), Associate Professor (2011–2017) and Professor (2017–). In 2009 he was a visiting research fellow at IBM's T. J. Watson Research Center. His honours include:
- Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar Prize in Physical Sciences (2012)
- Infosys Prize in Physical Sciences (2020)
- B. M. Birla Science Prize
- DAE Raja Ramanna Prize Lecture
- Oxford Instruments Young NanoScientist Award
- Prof. C.N.R. Rao Bangalore India Nano Science Award (per his group site)

(Wikipedia, group site.)

## Research footprint

His group studies structural, electrical and magnetic properties of nanoscale systems. Its work runs from fundamental quantum effects on charge and spin states, to carbon-based electronics, critical behaviour in smart materials, and new sensing schemes with nano/micro-electromechanical devices (group site). Specific themes:
- transport in 2D electron systems in semiconductors
- low-frequency noise as a probe of disorder and dynamics
- graphene and van der Waals heterostructures
- optoelectronics of atomically thin semiconductors
- stability of metallic nanowires and nanoparticles

His most-cited paper, Roy et al., "Graphene–MoS2 hybrid structures for multifunctional photoresponsive memory devices" (*Nature Nanotechnology* 8, 2013; ~1,400 Crossref citations), was an early demonstration of functional graphene/TMD heterostructure devices. The 2020 Infosys citation credits him with developing atomically thin 2D semiconductors for electronic, thermoelectric and optoelectronic devices. Recent highlights include universality in the quantum-critical flow of charge and heat in ultraclean graphene (*Nat. Phys.* 21, 2025) and graphene on moiré TMD superlattices (*Nano Lett.* 2024). His focus has moved from semiconductor 2D electron gases and noise (Cambridge era) toward van der Waals quantum materials and devices.
