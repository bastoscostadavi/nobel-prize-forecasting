## Biography

Amanda Weltman was born in 1979 in Cape Cod, Massachusetts, and grew up in Johannesburg and Cape Town. She studied at the University of Cape Town and completed her PhD in theoretical physics at Columbia University in 2007 under Brian Greene. She was a postdoc at the Centre for Theoretical Cosmology in Cambridge before returning to UCT, where INSPIRE lists her from 2009. Her honours include the South African Women in Science award for best emerging young researcher (2009), the Royal Society of South Africa's Meiring Naude Medal (2011), UCT young researcher awards (2010), a Next Einstein Fellowship (2016–17), and a South African Research Chair (Tier 2 2016–2020, Tier 1 2021–2025) (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Weltman is best known for the 2004 "chameleon" papers with Justin Khoury (Phys. Rev. D and Phys. Rev. Lett.). They proposed a scalar field whose mass depends on the local matter density, so a light field linked to dark energy could avoid local tests of gravity. These are her most cited works. She then helped turn the idea into laboratory tests: she joined Fermilab's GammeV experiment, which set early bounds on chameleons (2008), and its successor GammeV-CHASE (2010), which helped start the laboratory search for dark energy (Wikipedia). She has written roadmap papers on fundamental physics, including "Fundamental physics with the Square Kilometre Array" (lead author, 2018), the black-hole and gravitational-wave roadmap (2018), and cosmic-tension reviews ("Cosmology intertwined", 2022; CosmoVerse, 2025). Her recent focus is fast radio bursts as cosmological probes, including intergalactic dispersion measures, the distance-duality relation, primordial black holes and MeerKAT FRB and pulsar observations (2026 papers).

## Public statements and values

On why she became a physicist, Wikipedia quotes her: "understanding the way the Universe worked was just about the coolest job anyone could have." She has said that being raised in a family largely free of gender stereotypes helped her, and that exposure to such stereotypes in adolescence often keeps girls from science (Wikipedia, paraphrase).
