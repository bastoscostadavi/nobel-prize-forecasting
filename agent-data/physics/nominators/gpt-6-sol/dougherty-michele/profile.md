## Biography

Michele Karen Dougherty was born in 1962 in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. She studied at the University of Natal and received her PhD there in 1988 for work on wave–particle interactions in dispersive and anisotropic media. After a fellowship in Germany in applied mathematics, she joined Imperial College London in 1991. She became Professor of Space Physics in 2004 and was Head of the Department of Physics from 2018 to 2024. Her honours include the IOP Chree Medal (2007), the Royal Society Hughes Medal (2008), FRS (2012), a Royal Society Research Professorship (2014), the RAS Gold Medal for geophysics (2017), a CBE (2018), the IOP Richard Glazebrook Medal (2018) and AGU Fellowship (2019). She chaired the UK Space Agency's Science Programme Advisory Committee (2014–2016). She became the sixteenth Astronomer Royal in July 2025, the first woman in the office. She was IOP President from October 2025 and stepped aside in January 2026.

## Research footprint

Dougherty is an instrument-led planetary scientist. She worked on the Ulysses magnetometer team for Jupiter and was a Guest Investigator on Galileo data. She then became principal investigator of the Cassini magnetometer. Her best-known result is "Identification of a Dynamic Atmosphere at Enceladus with the Cassini Magnetometer" (Science 2006). Magnetic-field perturbations there led Cassini to fly closer to the moon, which then revealed a plume containing water and hydrocarbons. The Hughes Medal citation credits this "innovative use of magnetic field data" with changing views of the role of planetary moons. She is principal investigator of J-MAG on ESA's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer (JUICE) and co-authored the JUICE mission definition paper (Grasset et al., Planetary and Space Science 2013). Her work spans magnetospheric physics, moon–magnetosphere interactions and the search for subsurface oceans.

## Public statements and values

On the Enceladus flybys, Wikipedia quotes her as saying: "I watched the data coming back with my heart in my mouth because if we had messed up no one would have ever believed me again!" As STFC Executive Chair (January 2026), she wrote that "the STFC will need to focus our efforts on a more concentrated set of priorities, funded at a level that can be maintained over time" (Physics World). She has done wide public engagement, including The Life Scientific and Desert Island Discs.
