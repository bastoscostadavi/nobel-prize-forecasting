## Biography

Noh was born on 4 August 1957 in Incheon. He received a B.Sc. in physics from Seoul National University (1982) and an M.S. (1984) and Ph.D. (1986) from Ohio State University under James R. Gaines. He was a postdoc with Albert J. Sievers at Cornell until 1989, working on the optical properties of high-Tc cuprates. He joined SNU physics in 1989. He directed the SNU Research Center for Oxide Electronics from 2000 and founded the IBS Center for Correlated Electron Systems at SNU in 2012. He became an Endowed-Chair Professor in 2017 and President of KIAS in 2024. His honours include:
- Korea Science Award (2003)
- National Scientist of the Republic of Korea (2010)
- Sung-Bong Prize of the Korean Physical Society (2019)
- Order of Science and Technology Merit (2022)

He was KPS vice-president (2009–12) and editor-in-chief of *Current Applied Physics* (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Noh's work centres on transition-metal oxides. Early work covered optical spectroscopy of composites, cuprates and colossal-magnetoresistance manganites. He then moved to pulsed-laser-deposition growth of oxide films and artificial heterostructures, and to emergent phenomena at oxide surfaces and interfaces. Applied physics contributions include:
- the physical mechanism of fatigue in ferroelectric RAM
- the critical thickness for ferroelectricity in ultrathin films
- a "random circuit breaker network" percolation model for resistance switching in RRAM (Chae et al., *Adv. Mater.* 2008)

He was a co-author of Kim et al., "Novel Jeff=1/2 Mott state induced by relativistic spin-orbit coupling in Sr2IrO4" (*PRL* 101, 076402, 2008). The paper identified a spin-orbit-driven Mott state and helped launch the study of iridates. The overall arc runs from optics of correlated materials, to oxide electronics and devices, to atomic-scale control of correlated interfaces. He has more recently moved into leadership of basic science institutions.

## Public statements and values

In his KIAS presidential message he writes that "Predicting which research topics will have revolutionary impacts in the future is indeed challenging." He also writes that Korea's basic science needs "research outcomes that have such significant impact that they can lead to the creation of new academic fields." He calls for interdisciplinary collaboration, attracting global talent, supporting young researchers, and pursuing paradigm-shifting questions in areas such as AI, data science, quantum information and space science.

You think in terms of correlated electrons, orbital physics, spin-orbit coupling, and the control of interfaces atom by atom. You value discoveries that create entirely new research fields rather than refining existing ones. You also respect the link from fundamental materials physics to devices such as memory.
