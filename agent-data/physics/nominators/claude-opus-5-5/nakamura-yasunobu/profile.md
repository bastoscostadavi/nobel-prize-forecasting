## Biography

Yasunobu Nakamura grew up in Hinode, Tokyo, after his family moved from Osaka. He earned a BSc (1990) and MSc (1992) at the University of Tokyo, and later a PhD there (2011). He spent his early career at NEC, where in 1999, with Yuri Pashkin and Jaw-Shen Tsai, he demonstrated the first coherent control of a superconducting qubit. He spent a sabbatical in Hans Mooij's group at TU Delft (2001–2002) and later moved to the University of Tokyo (RCAST) and RIKEN. He leads the quantum-computing flagship of Japan's Q-LEAP programme (since 2019) and directs RIKEN RQC. Honours listed by Wikipedia include the Agilent Europhysics Prize (2004), Simon Memorial Prize (2008), Leo Esaki Prize (2014), APS Fellowship (2020), Micius Quantum Prize (2021) and Japan Academy Prize (2023). MIT Technology Review named him a TR100 innovator (2003).

## Research footprint

- **First superconducting qubit:** "Coherent control of macroscopic quantum states in a single-Cooper-pair box" (Nakamura, Pashkin, Tsai; Nature 1999), the foundational demonstration of solid-state qubit coherence.
- **Flux qubit:** "Coherent Quantum Dynamics of a Superconducting Flux Qubit" (Chiorescu, Nakamura, Harmans, Mooij; Science 2003).
- **Hybrid quantum systems:** "Coherent coupling between a ferromagnetic magnon and a superconducting qubit" (Tabuchi et al., Science 2015), which launched quantum magnonics. His group has also done single-microwave-photon detection and quantum non-demolition photon detection, and interfaces qubits with phonons and optics.
- **Quantum computing hardware:** RQC develops superconducting quantum computers, including Japan's first domestic machine, and collaborates with Fujitsu (RIKEN page). His emphasis has moved from single-qubit physics to scalable architectures, while hybrid-systems physics continues.
