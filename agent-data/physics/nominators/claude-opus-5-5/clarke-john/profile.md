## Biography

Born 10 February 1942 in Cambridge, England; attended The Perse School. BA in physics from Christ's College, Cambridge (1964). PhD (1968) at the Royal Society Mond Laboratory under Brian Pippard; he was among the first students of Darwin College. Postdoc at UC Berkeley, then assistant (1969), associate (1971) and full professor of physics (1973–2010); faculty scientist at LBNL from 1969. Fellow of the Royal Society (1986), international member of the US NAS (2012), American Philosophical Society (2017). Awards include a Sloan Fellowship (1970), a Guggenheim Fellowship (1977), California Scientist of the Year (1987), the Keithley Award (1998), the Comstock Prize (1999), the Hughes Medal (2004), the Fritz London Memorial Award, the Micius Quantum Prize (2021) and the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics. Honorary Fellow of Christ's and Darwin Colleges, Cambridge.

## Research footprint

As a PhD student he built the SLUG (Superconducting Low-inductance Undulatory Galvanometer), a superconducting voltmeter. At Berkeley he became a leading developer of SQUIDs, described by Steven Girvin as "the godfather of superconducting electronics". In 1984–85, with graduate student John Martinis and postdoc Michel Devoret, he demonstrated macroscopic quantum tunnelling of the phase in a current-biased Josephson junction and, using microwave spectroscopy, quantised energy levels. This tested predictions by Anthony Leggett and laid groundwork for superconducting qubits. Clarke calls it "the grandfather of qubits". His later work applies SQUIDs to ultralow-field NMR and MRI, geophysics, nondestructive evaluation and biosensing. For the Axion Dark Matter eXperiment (ADMX) he developed low-noise SQUID-based microwave amplifiers, a class of device later used for qubit readout. Throughout, his work has combined device physics, noise theory and measurement science.
