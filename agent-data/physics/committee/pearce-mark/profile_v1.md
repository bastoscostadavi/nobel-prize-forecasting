## Biography

Pearce was born on 29 March 1970 in the United Kingdom. He did a PhD in experimental particle physics at the University of Birmingham and CERN, then moved to Sweden in July 1996 as a guest researcher at KTH. He became professor of astroparticle physics at KTH in 2007 and in 2008 was Director of Undergraduate Studies in the Physics Department. In 2010 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded him the Wallmark Prize "for his inventiveness in instrument development that has resulted in new, noted findings on the occurrence of antimatter in cosmic radiation" (translated from Swedish Wikipedia). He was elected to the Academy's Class for physics on 15 January 2020, when he was also deputy Head of the School of Engineering Sciences at KTH.

## Research / intellectual footprint

- **Instrument-building experimentalist.** In 2008 he said: "I'm an experimentalist and build instruments which are sent into space on-board satellites and high altitude balloons."
- **Cosmic-ray antimatter (PAMELA).** He is a co-author of the PAMELA satellite papers, including the anomalous cosmic-ray positron fraction (2009, about 2,200 citations in OpenAlex), antiproton/proton ratios and precise proton, helium and electron spectra.
- **Gamma-ray astronomy.** He is a co-author of the Fermi Large Area Telescope instrument paper (2009) and of e-ASTROGAM mission studies (2017–18).
- **Hard X-ray polarimetry.** He led PoGOLite and PoGO+, balloon polarimeters flown from Esrange. They produced polarization results on the Crab (Scientific Reports, 2017) and Cygnus X-1 (MNRAS Letters, 2018). He then led Sweden's contribution to XL-Calibur (Crab measurements published in 2025).
- **Current plans.** KTH reports in 2025 that he wants a small-satellite gamma-ray polarimeter to study gamma-ray bursts and black-hole formation.
- **Shift over time.** His work moved from accelerator particle physics, to satellite cosmic-ray spectroscopy, to leading his own polarimetry missions. The throughline is detector development.

## Public statements and values

- **2023 prize, attosecond physics:** "there are as many attoseconds in a second as there are seconds which have passed since the Big Bang, 13.8 billion years ago" (AP via PBS).
- **2025 prize (to AP):** "Quantum computers is one very sort of obvious use, but they're also can be used for quantum sensors ... and perhaps also for cryptography."
- **On basic research (KTH, 2025):** "Discoveries in basic research have benefited society in the past in ways that are very difficult to predict."
- **On polarimetry (KTH, 2025):** measuring gamma-ray polarization gives "significantly higher diagnostic value" and would be "groundbreaking and unique".
- **Describing his field (2008):** astroparticle physics studies "the Universe using subatomic particles rather than light".

## Connections

- He works in the PAMELA collaboration, the Fermi-LAT collaboration and the XL-Calibur collaboration. KTH says his gamma-ray work involves "collaborating with groups in several locations around the world".
- He is at the KTH particle and astroparticle physics group at AlbaNova.
- He was elected to KVA in the same 2020 intake as Andreas Strömbergsson and others. His committee colleagues include Olle Eriksson (2025 Chair) and Göran Johansson.
