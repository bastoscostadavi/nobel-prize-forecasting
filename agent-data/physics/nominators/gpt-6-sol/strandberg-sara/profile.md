## Biography

Full name Sara Kristina Strandberg (INSPIRE also lists the earlier name variant Sara Lager). She did her PhD in physics at Stockholm University (2002–2007), advised by Barbro Åsman and Christophe Clément according to INSPIRE. Her thesis (2007) was on top-quark pair production cross-section measurements and an estimate of the D0 silicon detector lifetime at the Fermilab Tevatron. She was a postdoctoral fellow at UC Berkeley (2007–2010), then a junior research fellow (2010–2011), senior lecturer (2011–2020) and professor (since 2020) in the Stockholm University Physics Department (ORCID). From 2018 to 2023 she led the SHIFT project, a five-year Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation project (SU profile). No academy memberships or major named prizes were found.

## Research footprint

- **Tevatron (D0), 2002–2007.** Measurements of the top-quark pair production cross section in several channels (lepton+track, eμ, b-tagged lepton+jets), plus work on the silicon tracker.
- **ATLAS, from about 2007.** Early detector work on the pixel detector optical links and commissioning (2008 proceedings), then tracking, vertexing and b-tagging performance (2012 proceedings).
- **Analysis infrastructure.** Co-author of work on systematics-aware analysis workflows for the ATLAS Run-2 analysis model (2015).
- **Physics searches.** Her SU profile says she searches for extensions of the Standard Model that could explain dark matter, the matter-antimatter asymmetry and the Higgs-mass fine-tuning problem. Her present focus is Higgs-boson pair production, to measure the Higgs self-coupling. She links this to the shape of the Higgs potential and whether the electroweak phase transition could have been first-order. She also searches for the supersymmetric partner of the top quark.
- **Phenomenology.** The SHIFT project developed and searched for top-partner models. A related small-collaboration paper is "Electroweak signatures of gauge-mediated supersymmetry breaking in multiple hidden sectors" (JHEP 2021, with Buarque Franzosi, Ferretti and Riefel).

The trajectory runs from top-quark precision physics at the Tevatron, through detector and performance work, to Higgs and BSM searches at the LHC.
