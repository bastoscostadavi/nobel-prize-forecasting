## Biography

Kaspi was born in Austin, Texas, and moved to Canada at age seven. She earned a B.Sc. at McGill (1989) and a Ph.D. at Princeton (1993) under Joseph Taylor, with the thesis "Applications of pulsar timing." After postdoctoral work at Caltech and JPL and a junior faculty post at MIT, she joined McGill in 1999. There she held one of McGill's first Canada Research Chairs and became Lorne Trottier Professor of Astrophysics (2006) and Distinguished James McGill Professor. She founded the McGill Space Institute in 2015 and has directed CIFAR's Gravity and the Extreme Universe program since 2017. Her honours include:

- Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) and member of the US National Academy of Sciences (both 2010);
- Herzberg Canada Gold Medal (2016, the first woman to receive it);
- Companion of the Order of Canada (2016);
- Shaw Prize in Astronomy and the Royal Society's Bakerian Medal (2021);
- Albert Einstein World Award of Science (2022).

## Research footprint

Kaspi's thesis work on Arecibo timing of millisecond pulsars produced an early upper limit on the stochastic gravitational-wave background using pulsar timing. That method underlies later pulsar-timing arrays such as NANOGrav. Using RXTE, her group showed that soft gamma repeaters and anomalous X-ray pulsars can both be explained as magnetars. Her group also reported the first "anti-glitch" in the magnetar 1E 2259+586 (*Nature*, 2013). Other results include:

- Chandra observations tying a pulsar to the historical supernova remnant G11.2–0.3;
- a role in discovering the fastest-spinning known pulsar, PSR J1748–2446ad;
- evidence of pulsar "recycling."

She is a lead scientist of the CHIME/FRB project, which has detected hundreds of fast radio bursts. That work led *Nature* to name her one of its "10 people who mattered" in 2019. Her work has moved from radio timing, to X-ray studies of magnetars, to large-survey radio transient astronomy.
