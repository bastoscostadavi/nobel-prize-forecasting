## Biography

Born 5 March 1953 in Paris. He trained as an engineer at the École nationale supérieure des télécommunications (1975) and earned a PhD in 1982 at Orsay (now Paris-Saclay), working at CEA Saclay. From 1982 to 1984 he was a postdoc in John Clarke's group at UC Berkeley. He then co-founded the Quantronics group at CEA Saclay with Daniel Esteve and Cristian Urbina. He was Professor of Applied Physics at Yale from 2002 and held the Chair of Mesoscopic Physics at the Collège de France (2007–2012). Since about 2023–2024 he has been at UCSB and Google Quantum AI. His honours include the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics (shared with Clarke and Martinis), the Comstock Prize (2024) and the Micius Prize (2021). He is a member of the French Academy of Sciences, the US National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

At Berkeley in 1984–85, Devoret, Clarke and Martinis showed macroscopic quantum tunnelling and quantised energy levels in a current-biased Josephson junction. This is the work the 2025 prize cites. At Saclay he helped develop single-electron devices and the "quantronium" charge-phase qubit. At Yale, working with Schoelkopf and Girvin, he took part in developing the transmon and circuit quantum electrodynamics. He also introduced the fluxonium qubit (2009), built Josephson parametric amplifiers that operate near the quantum limit, and in 2019 published an experiment that caught and reversed a quantum jump in mid-flight. Over his career his focus has moved from basic tests of macroscopic quantum mechanics, to engineered quantum hardware, and now to quantum error correction and large-scale superconducting processors at Google.

## Public statements and values

- On the 1980s Berkeley work: "working on a fundamental question of physics in a way that was totally free" (NobelPrize.org telephone interview, 2025).
- "I would say that a fundamental discovery really becomes true when actually you can apply it to something concrete" (same interview). He said he admired scientists who did fundamental and applied research at the same time, and named John Clarke as his model.
- He said the path "from an initial discovery to the start of an industry" takes many decades (same interview).
- The title of his Collège de France inaugural lecture was "From the Atom to Quantum Machines" (Wikipedia).

## Connections

- Long-standing collaborators include John Clarke (his postdoctoral mentor) and John Martinis, his co-laureates. Others are Daniel Esteve and Cristian Urbina (Saclay Quantronics), Robert Schoelkopf and Steven Girvin (Yale), and Hans Mooij (Delft, where he made a 1996 research stay).
- Doctoral advisor: Neil S. Sullivan. The work was done in Anatole Abragam's orbit at CEA Saclay.
- Institutional networks: CEA Saclay, Yale Quantum Institute, Collège de France, UCSB, Google Quantum AI.
