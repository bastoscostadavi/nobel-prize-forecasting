## Biography

Adhikari was born in Ohio in 1974 to Bengali immigrants from India, and he grew up in Cape Canaveral, Florida (Wikipedia). He took a BS at the University of Florida in 1998, where he worked with David Reitze. His MIT PhD (2004) was supervised by Rainer Weiss, with a thesis titled "Sensitivity and Noise Analysis of 4 km Laser Interferometric Gravitational Wave Antennae". He received the first LIGO thesis prize in 2005. He joined LIGO at Caltech as a postdoc, became an assistant professor in 2006 and a full professor in 2012 (Caltech page). As a LIGO member he shared the collaboration-level 2016 Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics and Gruber Cosmology Prize, and the 2017 Princess of Asturias Award, Bruno Rossi Prize and RAS group award. He received the Einstein Medal of the University of Bern (2017) and the 2019 New Horizons in Physics Prize, the latter with Lisa Barsotti and Matt Evans. He is an APS Fellow, a founding member of LIGO-India and a juror for the Infosys Prize in physical sciences (Caltech page; Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

Adhikari has worked on LIGO interferometers since 1997. As a graduate student he tackled noise sources, feedback loops and subsystems, and helped reduce noise in all three initial LIGO interferometers (Wikipedia). His Caltech group develops detector technology for fundamental physics: gravitational waves, dark matter and near-field gravity. It works on techniques that go beyond standard quantum limits, including squeezing, cryogenic and crystalline mirror concepts, mechanical oscillators, nonlinear optics and high-efficiency photodetection. With theorist Kathryn Zurek he developed tabletop interferometer concepts to look for signatures of quantum gravity in spacetime fluctuations. Recent papers include LIGO O4 detector characterisation, the "LIGO A#" upgrade design (2026) and quantum back-action evasion on kilogram-scale mirrors (2026). Over time he has moved from making initial LIGO work toward designing future detectors and new tabletop tests of quantum spacetime. He has also explored machine learning for detector control, as covered in Caltech news.
