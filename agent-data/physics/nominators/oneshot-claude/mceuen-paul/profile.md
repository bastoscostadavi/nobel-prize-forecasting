## Biography

McEuen was born in Oklahoma in 1963. He took a BS in engineering physics at the University of Oklahoma in 1985 and a PhD in applied physics at Yale in 1991. After a postdoc at MIT (1990–91), he was assistant professor (1992–96) and then associate professor (1996–2000) of physics at UC Berkeley. He moved to Cornell in 2001, where he became Goldwin Smith and later John A. Newman Professor. He now holds emeritus status. His honours include:
- Sloan and Packard Fellowships;
- the Agilent Europhysics Prize (2001);
- APS Fellowship (2003);
- election to the NAS (2011);
- election to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (2015).

He wrote the chapter on nanostructures in the 8th edition of Kittel's *Introduction to Solid State Physics*. He also wrote a thriller novel, *Spiral* (2011), which the International Thriller Writers named debut novel of the year (Cornell page; Wikipedia).

## Research footprint

McEuen made his name in the electrical properties of carbon nanotubes. His work covered single-electron transport, Luttinger-liquid behaviour, nanotube transistors and scanning-probe imaging of nanotube devices. He then broadened to nanoelectromechanics, graphene membranes and molecular electronics (Wikipedia, citing his lab pages). From around the mid-2010s his group turned to what the department page calls "the science and technology of nanostructures" at the boundary with machines. In collaboration with Itai Cohen, he builds cell-sized, electronically integrated microscopic robots and origami-like actuators. Recent co-authored work includes wafer-scale heterogeneous integration (BLAST, 2023), bifurcation-based multistate machines (2023) and "multistable energy landscapes for adaptive microscopic machines" (2026). The through-line is building nanoscale devices whose physics can be measured and then put to work.

## Public statements and values

No direct interview quotes were retrieved in this session. His public record includes a role in scientific integrity. In 2002 he found duplicated noise across figures in Jan Hendrik Schön's papers. With Lydia Sohn, Lynn Loo and others he compiled the evidence and sent it to Schön, his co-authors and the editors of *Science* and *Nature*, which helped trigger Bell Labs/Lucent's investigation (Wikipedia, Schön scandal). He also communicates science to the public through fiction. Both point to a strong commitment to rigour and reproducibility and to engagement beyond academia (inference).
