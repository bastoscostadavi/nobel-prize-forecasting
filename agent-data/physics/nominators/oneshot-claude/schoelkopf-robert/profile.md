## Biography

Schoelkopf was born in Manhattan in 1964. He earned an AB in physics from Princeton (1986, cum laude). From 1986 to 1988 he was a cryogenic and electrical engineer at NASA Goddard's Laboratory for High-Energy Astrophysics, where he developed low-temperature radiation detectors. He took his PhD at Caltech in 1995 under Thomas G. Phillips, with a thesis on noise in Josephson-effect mixers for submillimetre detection. He came to Yale in 1995 as a postdoc with Daniel Prober. At Yale he became assistant professor in 1998 and professor in 2003, then William A. Norton Professor (2013) and Sterling Professor. He has directed the Yale Quantum Institute since 2014. His honours include:
- the Keithley Award (2009);
- the John Stewart Bell Prize (2013, with Michel Devoret);
- the Fritz London Memorial Prize (2014, with Devoret and John Martinis);
- the Max Planck Research Award (2014);
- NAS membership (2015);
- the Connecticut Medal of Science (2017);
- the NAS Comstock Prize in Physics (2024).

## Research footprint

With Prober, Schoelkopf invented the radio-frequency single-electron transistor, an electrometer that resolves sub-electron charge on nanosecond timescales. With Steven Girvin and Michel Devoret he developed circuit quantum electrodynamics. In this architecture superconducting qubits couple to microwave resonators, and a resonator "bus" was used in 2007 to transfer quantum information between distant qubits on a chip. He co-invented the transmon qubit (2007, Yale/Sherbrooke) and later the 3D transmon. In 2009 his team demonstrated a two-qubit superconducting processor running simple quantum algorithms. Later work has centred on bosonic encodings in microwave cavities, quantum error correction, and hybrid systems. "Schoelkopf's law" is the observation that superconducting-qubit coherence times improved about tenfold every three years (Wikipedia). His recent preprints, such as work on granular-aluminium resonators in 2026, continue materials and loss studies for scalable hardware.
