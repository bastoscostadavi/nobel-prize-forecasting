My pick is **Michael Berry, possibly sharing the prize with Yakir Aharonov**, for revealing how quantum particles are affected by the geometry of their evolution and by electromagnetic potentials.

Berry’s **geometric phase**, introduced in 1984, became a foundational idea across quantum physics, optics, and topological materials. The **Aharonov–Bohm effect** showed that electromagnetic potentials can affect quantum interference even where the particles encounter no magnetic field. Both are well-established discoveries with decades of experimental confirmation—the kind of mature, fundamental work Nobel committees often recognize.

My strongest alternative is **Ignacio Cirac, Peter Zoller, and Immanuel Bloch**, for pioneering quantum simulation with controllable atomic systems.

That’s a low-confidence prediction: these are compelling candidates, but predicting *which year* the committee recognizes a discovery is much harder than identifying Nobel-worthy work.
